Seer infrastructure on AWS — plan and cost

2026-11-15 · Live version: claude.ai artifact

At a glance

About $7–11 a month at 1M views, and about $87–127 for the first year, in Frankfurt with no NAT gateway.

  • Pipeline: every hour from 08:00 to 22:00, scrape → S3 raw batch → ingest → Postgres → stories → static pages on S3 and CloudFront.
  • Network: only scrape needs the internet, and it runs outside the VPC. The three VPC Lambdas need only S3 (through a free gateway endpoint) and Aurora.
  • The record: every fetched page is kept gzipped in S3 and tiered down to Deep Archive. It's deleted after 2 years. Postgres keeps 3 years of history.
  • Updates are bumped, not reprocessed. Only new articles are embedded; an edited article just raises its story.
  • Tested locally: all four Lambdas run as real container images. The prototype is merged in PR #16.
  • Ads, best guess: about $240–1,200 a month at 1M views, depending on the mk/fr mix and AdSense approval.

Architecture

Only the scrape Lambda talks to news sites, and it runs outside the VPC, so it needs no NAT. Everything inside the VPC reaches S3 through a free gateway endpoint and Aurora directly. Readers never touch the database; they get static pages from CloudFront.

How an hourly run works

EventBridge Scheduler starts one Step Functions run at the top of every hour from 08:00 to 22:00, Skopje time. That's 15 runs a day, about 450 a month. Scrape and ingest run per edition, in parallel; stories and export run once both editions are in.

  1. Scrape (Lambda 1, outside the VPC). One invocation per edition. It reads state/{edition}.json.gz to see what's already stored, then fetches only what's new:
    • WordPress sites (mk): posts created or edited since that site's last good run (modified_after). An hour usually brings a few posts per site.
    • News-sitemap sites (fr): article pages it hasn't seen, plus a capped re-check of recent articles to catch edits.
    • Everything fetched, raw API JSON and raw HTML, goes to S3 as one gzipped file per edition per run, with a small manifest of counts and errors. Scrape never touches the database.
  2. Ingest (Lambda 2, in the VPC). Reads that file, extracts text, images and categories, and upserts articles into Postgres. Each re-fetched article is compared with the stored copy: a new title, or more than 5% of the text changed, marks it as updated. Ingest then rewrites the state file for the next scrape. Ingesting the same file twice changes nothing.
  3. Stories (Lambda 3, in the VPC, model in the image). Embeds new articles only and adds each to an open story, or starts a new one.
  4. Export (Lambda 4, in the VPC). Renders Top and Latest pages per edition and uploads them to the site bucket. CloudFront serves them with a 5-minute cache.

Updates are bumped, not reprocessed. An edited article keeps its first embedding and its story. Its story only gets a new activity time, so it rises in Top. Nothing is re-embedded or re-clustered.

The raw files are the record. If extraction or clustering improves, past runs can be re-ingested from S3 without scraping the sites again.

Storage and retention

S3 raw bucket: the record, kept 2 years

Prefix Holds Retention
raw/{edition}/{YYYY}/{MM}/{DD}/{HH}/{run}.jsonl.gz Everything fetched in one run: raw WordPress JSON or raw HTML, one record per line, gzipped Deleted after 730 days
runs/{edition}/{run}.json Manifest: counts, sizes, errors, and the ingest result Deleted after 730 days
state/{edition}.json.gz What scrape has already seen in the last 48 h Overwritten every run
  • Compressed when written. Scrape gzips the batch before uploading. Compressing after processing would cost a second write and a delete per object, and S3 charges per request. Ingest reads the gzipped file directly.
  • One object per edition per run, about 900 a month. Intelligent-Tiering never moves objects under 128 KB; they stay in the most expensive tier [12]. Batching keeps objects above that size and requests low.
  • Path to Glacier: S3 Intelligent-Tiering moves an object to Infrequent Access after 30 days without reads and to Archive Instant Access after 90 [12]. We turn on the two optional archive tiers: Archive Access after 90 days and Deep Archive Access after 180 days. These are priced like Glacier Flexible Retrieval and Glacier Deep Archive.
  • Reading archived files needs a restore first, which takes hours. That's fine for a record, but re-ingests should happen within 90 days.
  • Expiry: a lifecycle rule deletes raw/ and runs/ after 730 days.
  • Config files: infra/s3-raw-intelligent-tiering.json and infra/s3-raw-lifecycle.json in the prototype.

Postgres (Aurora): 3 years of history

  • Kept 3 years: articles (title, text, image link, categories, update history), embeddings, stories and their members. A monthly job deletes anything older.
  • Growth: about 14 KB per article across all tables, measured on 2,389 articles. That's about 1,180 articles a day, or about 6 GB a year and about 18 GB after 3 years.
  • Embeddings are about a third of that. They're only needed while a story is open (48 h). Dropping them after 30 days would keep 3 years at about 12 GB. It's an open decision, because old vectors would allow "related past stories" later.
  • Backups: Aurora's automated backups. The raw bucket is the deeper record: the database can be rebuilt from it.

  • Backfills are the long runs. A 48 h French backfill takes 6–7 minutes, still under Lambda's 15-minute limit.

Revenue with Google ads (best guess)

I found no published AdSense rates for news websites in France or North Macedonia; searches only turned up YouTube figures, which don't carry over. The rates below are my estimate from general industry knowledge. Treat them as a range to test with real traffic, not a forecast.

Readers Revenue per 1,000 page views (after consent refusals and ad blockers) 500k views 800k views 200k views
France (fr) $0.80–2.00 $400–1,000 — $160–400
North Macedonia (mk) $0.10–0.40 $50–200 $80–320 —
Total per month 50/50 split: about $450–1,200 80% mk, 20% fr: about $240–720
  • Ads don't change the infrastructure bill. Google serves the ads and their scripts, not our CloudFront.
  • Break-even is small. At about $10–15 a month, roughly 10–40k French views or 40–150k Macedonian views cover the bill.
  • Approval is the real risk. Google's policies bar ads on scraped content [15]. A page of headlines and outbound links can be rejected as "low value content". The pages need value of their own: grouped stories, source counts, timelines, edition pages, and ideally short original story lines.
  • French readers need a consent banner. Google's own consent tool is free. Readers who refuse get fewer, cheaper ads, which the fr range above assumes.
  • In North Macedonia, direct sales probably pay better. Selling banners to local advertisers, as established local portals do, likely beats AdSense at Balkan rates.

Notes and risks

Legal and access (these matter more than cost)

  • Images at 1M views. The pages load photos straight from the outlets' servers. At 10–20 images per view, that's 10–20M image requests a month on 20 outlets, and the no-referrer setting hides that they come from us. That isn't acceptable at this scale. Hosting thumbnails ourselves fixes the bandwidth problem but copies licensed photos. Decide before launch.
  • robots.txt must be obeyed before anything is public. The prototype ignores it for experiments only.
  • Ads change the legal picture. Google's policies bar ads next to scraped content [15], and an aggregator is exposed to that. Commercial use of French headlines and snippets also falls under the French publishers' neighbouring right (droit voisin).
  • The raw archive is a copy of publishers' pages, kept for 2 years for internal use only and never republished. EU text-and-data-mining rules let publishers opt out, and many French ones do. Check with counsel before launch.
  • No fixed outbound IP address. Without a NAT, scrape uses AWS's shared addresses. Sites that block AWS ranges will block us, and we can't ask an outlet to allowlist one IP.

Data quality

  • Edited articles are never re-embedded. That's cheap and stable. But a live blog that drifts to a new topic stays in its first story.
  • Le Figaro raises false "updated" flags (17 of 111 in testing). With update bumps, its stories would float to the top every hour. Normalize its text before comparing, or use a stricter ratio for that site.

Runtime

  • Overnight catch-up is automatic. Scrape asks each site for everything since its last good run, so the 08:00 run picks up the night with no special setting.
  • Backfills don't fit one Lambda. Embedding a 48 h backfill takes about 10 minutes, close to the 15-minute limit. Run backfills in batches.
  • Aurora resumes in about 15 s on each run. Connections retry for up to about a minute.
  • Local emulator limit: each local function container takes one call at a time, so the local runner queues them. Real Lambda scales out.

Operations

  • Re-ingesting old runs after 90 days needs a restore from the archive tiers first, which takes hours.
  • Alarms: a failed Step Functions run, and a site older than 2 hours.
  • Retention jobs: a monthly SQL delete for anything over 3 years old. S3 expiry is automatic.

Options we rejected

Option Why not
NAT gateway for Lambdas in the VPC About $44 a month in Frankfurt (hours, data and IPv4 [1][6]), just so two scrapers can reach the internet. It was 80% of the earlier design's bill.
NAT instance (a small EC2 instance) About $7–8 a month plus a server to patch. The same workaround, only smaller.
RDS Data API for the scrapers It would mean rewriting the database layer. Not needed once scrape only writes to S3.
Aurora with a public endpoint Lambda's addresses change, so the database would have to be open to the whole internet. It also brings back the $3.65 IPv4 charge.
IPv6-only outbound traffic (free) Only 10 of our 20 sources have IPv6 addresses [11].
One small VM running Docker Compose Still a fair choice: about $10–15 a month and closest to the prototype. But it never scales to zero, and it's a machine to patch and monitor.

Open decisions

  1. Images. Keep loading them from the outlets' servers, host our own thumbnails, or show none. At 1M views this is a bandwidth and legal question, not a cost question (see Notes and risks).
  2. Embedding retention. Keep vectors 3 years, about 18 GB, or drop them after 30 days, about 12 GB.
  3. French re-check cap. Re-check 5 or 20 recent articles per site each hour. It sets scrape time and how quickly French edits show.
  4. Ads. AdSense, or direct sales to local advertisers. The site needs a consent banner either way for French readers.
  5. Region. Frankfurt or Paris. The prices here are Frankfurt.
  6. Infrastructure as code. CDK or Terraform.
  7. Domain. A .mk, .fr or neutral domain.
